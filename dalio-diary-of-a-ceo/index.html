Ray Dalio: "Classic Signs" of an AI Bubble — and What Comes After

The Bridgewater founder who called the 2008 crisis walks Steven Bartlett through the AI bubble, the 80-year "Big Cycle," and how ordinary people should position themselves.

Guest: Ray Dalio, founder, Bridgewater Associates Host: Steven Bartlett Published: July 30, 2026 Runtime: 1:30:17

The headline claim: yes, it's a bubble

Asked directly whether he agrees with investor Jeremy Grantham that we are staring at an AI bubble — possibly "the biggest investment bubble in American history" — Dalio's answer is blunt: "He's right." He is careful to frame a bubble as a matter of degree, not a binary, but says today's market shows the classic signs: revolutionary technology, investors ignoring price, heavy borrowing against inflated paper wealth, an explosion of new stock issuance, and "weak hands" — unsophisticated investors piling in through leveraged vehicles like leveraged ETFs, "crapshooting."

"There's almost nothing that's easier to produce than stock." — Ray Dalio, on the supply side of every bubble

His template is 1929 and the 2000 dot-com bust: a genuinely transformative technology (electricity, cars, radio then; the internet in 2000; AI now) whose real future doesn't rescue investors who overpaid for it. The mechanics: paper wealth becomes loan collateral on the way up; when holders suddenly need cash — rate hikes, taxes, a shock — everyone sells at once, collateral collapses below the debt against it, spending contracts, and the downturn feeds on itself.

What pricks a bubble

  • Tighter money — rate rises make holding debt pay better than holding equity, and force leveraged holders to raise cash.
  • Wealth taxes or tax changes — anything that forces holders to sell wealth to get money.
  • Stock supply — a flood of issuance meets the demand. Bartlett's own anecdote lands here: a friend running an AI company just raised hundreds of millions "because it's easy now," planning to buy struggling competitors after the crash. Dalio's response: that raise itself is the supply that helps pop the bubble.

The Big Cycle: why this bubble is more dangerous than most

Dalio's core framework — the subject of his book The Changing World Order, based on 500 years of measurable data across countries — is that a bubble bursting is far worse when it lands on three other late-cycle conditions at once, which he says is exactly where the US and UK sit today:

  • Debt: governments are over-indebted with big deficits and shrinking borrowing capacity; debt service squeezes everything else out.
  • Internal conflict: large wealth gaps map onto left–right polarization; compromise breaks down. His marker for the UK: effectively a new prime minister in six of the last seven years.
  • External conflict: the world order is shifting — China is now a larger trading partner than the US for most countries, and US deterrent power is eroding.

Underneath all of it runs the one line that never reverses: technology keeps improving, because "you don't unlearn what you've learned." The 80-year order cycle and the ~6-year boom–recession cycles oscillate around that rising line.

What to do about it: diversification, not timing

Dalio refuses to time the top — "sophisticated investors have a real challenge even in timing a bubble" — and instead repeats his portfolio catechism. First, count your months: how long could you and your family live if no money came in? Secure that. Then diversify across assets that move differently, because balance cuts risk without cutting return.

AI, jobs, and who wins

Dalio's evolutionary frame: machines first replaced the body (tractors, factories), then routine mental work, and AI is now climbing into "higher and higher levels of thinking and reasoning." The winners are "the capitalists with the ideas that replace the workers" — the labor share of revenue falls while the owner share rises. He notes college graduates already struggle to find work: for many tasks, a graduate who needs training is now "more of a pain in the neck" than the AI. Two forces will hit employment together: the slow evolutionary replacement of tasks, and the sharp spike in unemployment when the bubble bursts and companies cut for survival.

His advice to a 16-year-old (or anyone repositioning)

  • Adaptability beats intelligence. "It's not the most intelligent people that are the most successful… it is those who are most adaptable." Don't bet on a specific job title — "make sure you know how to code" was safe advice until Claude Code arrived, he notes.
  • Partner with AI. For the foreseeable future, exceptional human intelligence working with AI is the cutting edge. Maximize your ability to learn and your usefulness.
  • Know your nature. Match your work to your temperament (his free ~30-minute assessment, PrinciplesYou). Make work and passion the same thing — "and don't forget about the money part."
  • Same skill, different market. Bartlett's addition: the identical skill is priced wildly differently across industries — driving an Uber vs. chauffeuring Ray Dalio. Dalio's corollary: the top of any category commands multiples of the average — 10% better can mean 2× the pay.
  • Money is not the goal. Past a basic level, there's little correlation between money and happiness; secure your months-of-runway floor, then pursue the pull.

Wealth taxes, the UK, and the world order

Wealth taxes: mechanically dangerous — forcing the wealthy to sell assets to pay the tax is precisely a bubble-pricking event, valuation is an administrative nightmare, and people leave (then come retroactive taxes, exit taxes, capital controls — "all of this has happened before"). He prefers reforms like taxing stepped-up basis at death, paired with productivity investment — education, not pure transfer payments. The UK he calls "the classic cycle: over-indebted, under-productive, and out of choices," heading toward restructuring — money-printing plus debt-maturity extension. His prescription: a strong middle, a bipartisan commission, shared pain. His advice to a young UK founder: "exist without borders" — base yourself in renaissance-city bright spots, and remember "a smart rabbit has three holes."

Geopolitics: the US–Iran war is, in his view, "a big mistake" that exposed — like Suez did for Britain — that US threats no longer carry. Asian allies now doubt the US would show up; China can exert power without war (a five-day Taiwan chip blockade would crash world markets). His base case is not a US–China hot war but a regional world order — an Americas sphere and an Asia-Pacific sphere — with Taiwan resolved by pressure rather than invasion. Each power's fate will be decided from within: education, budgets, civility, productivity.

"Now a light bulb goes off. Like the British at Suez — we didn't realize. Now we realize that that power no longer exists." — Ray Dalio, on what the Iran war revealed about US deterrence
