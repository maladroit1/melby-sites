Where the vineyard actually stands

Two independent research passes attacked the plan. One thing they said was right, one thing changed when the shade came to light, and three things are worth doing this year.

The frame that settles everything

Priorities, in order. Every decision below follows from this — and most of the earlier worry dissolves once it's written down.

1Healthy, beautiful vines The actual goal. This is a garden that makes wine, not a winery with a garden.
2Great wine — quality, not volume Which happens to align perfectly with thinning the crop hard.
3Sparkling — a fun side experiment Not a programme, not a deadline, not the measure of success.
Why this matters more than it sounds

The research verdict was "the wrong instrument for a production project." True — and irrelevant, because production was never the goal. Written this way, the vines stay, nothing gets replanted, and the pressure comes off.

The shade changes the diagnosis

The case against this site was too hot — the acid will collapse and the fruit will sunburn. Partial shade attacks that directly.

✓ What the shade gives you

  • Berry temperature down as much as 4 °C
  • Holds acid — around +1 g/L retained
  • Sun burns off malic acid; shade protects it
  • Sunburn risk drops a lot
  • Makes this site behave more like a cool climate — which is exactly where Seyval belongs

✗ What it costs you

  • Grapes want 6–8+ hours direct sun; heavy ripening wants 10–12
  • Less sugar — ripening is slower
  • Powdery mildew runs 49–75% worse in shade — sun and heat suppress it
  • Shaded buds are less fruitful next year — the loss compounds
  • Harvest pushed later, toward frost
The honest headline

Good news: the "wrong climate" objection is much weaker than it looked. Acid retention is precisely what this grape needs, and shade delivers it.

But the problem flips. It is no longer heat and acid loss — it is light and ripening. Which probably also explains this year's 6 Brix better than crop load alone does.

The gap you're actually closing

The one question that sharpens all of this

Worth measuring once, properly

How many hours of direct sun do the vines get, and is the shade morning or afternoon? Stand at the row and note when sun reaches it and when it leaves — one day in July answers it for good.

What changed in the advice

Earlier adviceCorrected
Rebuild the trellis over 3 years, starting now Right system, wrong urgency. Learn to prune on the wire you have first.
Sparkling is the goal A side experiment. It was never stress-tested against this climate.
The dry climate protects you from mildew Overstated — shade cancels much of it. A real spray programme is needed.
6 Brix is because the vines are over-cropped Partly. Shade is likely the bigger cause.
Manage for heat: protect fruit, pick early Manage for light: expose leaves, cut crop harder, expect a later pick.

This year — three things

  1. Confirm what the grape actually is. Everything is built on a name from memory. The free test is at ripeness: skin should cling and taste neutral. If it slips off or smells like grape soda, the plan changes.
  2. Fix the weak side and thin hard. Check the drip on the weak row first — a whole side running weak is a system, not bad luck. Then cut the crop to what the leaves can actually ripen.
  3. Make one small batch of still wine and judge it honestly. Pick early to test whether the acid holds. If it's thin or flat, that's your answer — and it cost one season instead of three.

Parked, not cancelled

  • The multi-year trellis conversion
  • Traditional-method sparkling
  • Planting more vines
  • Replacing anything

Each becomes answerable after the grape is confirmed and one measured season is in the notebook.
