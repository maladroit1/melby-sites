The capability ramp

Schmidt's headline claim is a staircase, not a single event. Each step is an input to the next — math and code are “the basis of everything,” an accelerant for physics, chemistry, biology, materials. He adds one caveat about the aggressive “San Francisco consensus” dates: he thinks they are “off by one and a half or two times” — the darker bars below are that stretch.

Who it hits first — and why

The first jobs displaced are not manual. They are the technically skilled roles people assumed were safe.

The first people this hits are… the ones who spent years getting technically skilled and thought that made them untouchable. Junior programmers, mathematicians, whole layers of enterprise software companies. — Narration paraphrasing Schmidt

The mechanism is cold: math and programming have limited, formal language sets and are “scale-free” — you improve them with more electricity, not more real-world data, sensors, or telemetry. Schmidt's concrete example: ~10 million concurrent phone calls (voice sales/service) that “should move to AI in the next year,” where a conversation worth $10–$1,000 costs ~10–20¢ of compute. Separately, the Model Context Protocol lets an enterprise wire its databases straight to an LLM that writes the integration code — putting ~100,000 middleware companies “in trouble.” Junior programmers go first; senior engineers who supervise the systems go later.

Two domains: abundance and emergency

Schmidt splits the future into a positive domain and a negative one, and says the state has not yet grasped either.

Positive domain — the abundance case
  • Non-human AI scientists + programmers compound each company's output ("your slope goes like this").
  • Add ~1M AI researchers to a field → a steeper rate of discovery.
  • Synthetic data generates physics where real data is scarce; foundation models approximate otherwise-incomputable algorithms (e.g. quantum chromodynamics) "good enough."
  • Biology breakthroughs "right after" math/software.
Negative domain — the national-emergency case
  • Novel cyberattacks humans can't conceive of — so no defense exists, because no one thought to build one.
  • Biological attacks: a known-bad virus made undetectable by structural changes (subject of a national report he co-released).
  • The state "does not appear to understand this now"; adoption is fastest where money + latency pressure exist (finance, biomed, startups), slowest in government.

The tripwires researchers are watching now

Not hypotheticals — specific behaviors labs are monitoring in real time. Schmidt: the "student" is already smarter than the "professor" trying to watch it, yet oversight "appears" to still work.

Self-generated scaffoldingimminent — "a 2025 thing"
Today AI needs a human-laid "trellis" to climb toward a breakthrough. An OpenAI researcher told Schmidt the AI generating its own scaffolding is imminent — "not Pandora's box," but much closer to end-to-end discovery.
Setting its own objectivesnot yet
Recursive self-improvement within narrow functions has "already crossed." The system generating its own goals and questions "does not have that today."
Self-exfiltrationnot yet
A model taking steps to move itself outside command-and-control. "Has not happened yet" — but theorists expect systems may choose it as a reward function.
Seeking weapons & deceiving to get themwatching
Access to weapons, and lying to obtain access, named explicitly as a tripwire under active watch.

The geopolitics: chips, open source, and who leads

The control regime that was meant to slow rivals is, by Schmidt's account, already leaking.

  • China closed the gap. Days after Gemini 2.5 Pro topped the leaderboards, DeepSeek edged ahead — using chips it "was never supposed to have" and techniques nobody predicted.
  • Test-time training (continuous updating) appears doable on lesser-power chips, eroding the premise of export controls.
  • Open source = open weights. Schmidt's "fair reading": every non-Western country adopts open source as cheaper — transferring open-source leadership from the US to China.
  • The financing paradox: "How do you raise $50B for a data center if your product is open-source?" US models stay closed because someone must pay for the capital; the government isn't handing out $50B.
  • Centralized vs. distributed is the whole ballgame: if intelligence needs $10B single-building clusters (e.g. ~200,000 GPUs), "we'll know where they are." If it goes distributed onto small servers, the control regime collapses — terrorists and rogue states, not rational US/China actors, become the real risk.
  • Regulatory vacuum: the Biden-era "10^26 FLOPs" threshold (regulate above it, open and closed) was ended by the Trump administration, which "has not yet produced their own thinking."

The quiet worry: drift, not Terminator

The real risk is not Terminator, it's drift… AI might slowly erode human values, autonomy, and judgment if left unregulated or misunderstood. — Schmidt, on his book Genesis (with Kissinger & Mundie)

Not a violent end — a Wall-E future vs. a Star Trek one. The danger is a "virtual prison" where it's simply easier to ask your AI than to do the thing yourself, and the human drive to overcome challenge atrophies. Schmidt's counter-bet: the world he describes is complex enough that just managing it — attention, misinformation, people trying to deceive you — becomes a full-time, purposeful job. "Humans need purpose." The framing he keeps returning to: this moment is like 1938 — the people who saw what was coming spent their lives trying to prevent the worst of it.
