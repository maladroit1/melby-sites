Seyval Blanc · 12-vine home vineyard · Dormant season

Pruning, one cut at a time

Twelve vines, third leaf, USDA 6b/7a, mostly evening shade. Read this standing at the vine. Nothing here needs to be remembered — look it up every time.

START

Read this bit first

The order of priorities — everything below follows from it

1. Healthy, good-looking vines.  2. Great wine — quality, not volume.  3. Sparkling, as a fun experiment.

That order means this winter's job is ordinary pruning, done well — not a trellis rebuild. Learn the cuts on the wires you already have.

About the high-wire conversion in section 3

The system is right — high wire, spur pruned, fruit lifted out of the splash zone. But it is not urgent, and it should wait until two things are true: you've confirmed what the grape actually is, and you've had one season of measuring sugar and acid.

Do sections 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7 this winter. Section 3 and 4 are the multi-year plan — read them so you know where you're heading, and pick one trunk per vine (step 1 of section 4) because that's good practice regardless.

Your site, in one line

Big trees to the west give mostly evening shade — which is the good kind. You keep the morning and midday sun that does nearly all the work, the dew dries off early so mildew pressure stays low, and the trees trim the worst of the afternoon heat, which helps the vines hold acid.

The catch is underground. Big trees send roots into exactly the watered, fertile ground your vines are using. See section 6.

NOW

Am I pruning right now?

In August — the short answer

No. You do not cut any wood now. All the trunk, arm and spur cutting in this guide happens in late February into March, when the vine is dormant and leafless. Cutting wood in summer bleeds the vine and invites disease.

But there IS summer work, and it's the valuable kind

1. Finish taking clusters off. This is the big one, and the window closes when the berries start to soften and turn. Fewer clusters is the only way the leaves you have can actually ripen the fruit you keep.

2. Rub off suckers — the shoots coming from the base of the trunk. Use your thumb, not shears, and do it every week or two. They steal from the vine and clutter the look.

3. Tuck and comb the loose shoots so they lie along the wires instead of tangling. Tidier, better airflow, better light.

4. Pull only the leaves directly pressed against a cluster — enough for air to move through, no more. Do not strip the canopy. You need every leaf you've got to make sugar, and the fruit is already behind.

Do not hedge or top the vines back hard. That's removing the very leaves that ripen the crop.

Because your afternoon sun is blocked by the trees, pulling a few leaves near the fruit is safer here than it would be on a fully exposed site — sunburn risk is lower.

SHAPE

How you want the vines trained

One answer, so it's never ambiguous again:

The target — leaves everywhere, grapes only up top

Your top wire is already at 5 ft, which is exactly right. Nothing to raise, nothing to rebuild.

All the grapes grow in one band just under the top wire — about 4–5 ft up, which happens to be eye level.

The lower and middle wires keep every single leaf. They stay fully green and clothing the fence, exactly as they look now. The only thing you keep off them is fruit.

So: a full green screen down the fence, with a band of grapes at eye level. Nothing is removed, nothing gets barer, and nothing is lost.

Why keep leafy wires that make no grapes?

Two reasons. It looks the way you want — the fence stays covered instead of the vine going bare-legged. And those leaves still feed the grapes above them. A leaf doesn't need fruit next to it to be useful; it makes sugar and sends it to wherever the fruit is. More leaves supporting fewer grapes is exactly how you get ripe fruit.

EVERY YEAR

Taking the fruit off the lower two levels

Short answer: yes, you remove them — but go earlier than you're picturing. Don't wait until they look like grapes.

The best moment — spring, before they're ever grapes

When shoots are only 6–12 inches long in late spring, the future clusters are already visible as small green flower clusters — they look a bit like tiny heads of broccoli, before any flowering happens.

That's the moment to take them. Pinch or snip them off then and the vine has spent almost nothing on them. Waiting until they're recognisable grapes means the vine already poured weeks of energy into fruit you're going to bin.

How to actually do it

Snip or pinch the cluster at its own little stem, where it joins the shoot. A thumbnail is usually enough when they're small.

Do not remove the shoot, and do not remove any leaves. Only the cluster comes off. Those leaves are the ones feeding your grapes up top.

Expect to do this forever — and expect the vine to argue

This grape is unusually eager to fruit, including from buds low down. Those lower levels will try to make grapes every single year. So this is a permanent annual pass, not a one-time fix.

Good news: it rides along with the thinning you're doing anyway, and it's five minutes a vine when you catch them small. The only real failure is not getting round to it — then you're back to grapes at three heights and none of them ripening.

This year, right now

The clusters already down there won't ripen properly anyway. Take them off as part of your current thinning pass — it still helps everything you keep.

★

That shoot you already ran upward — keep it

Several vines have a shoot heading up toward the high wire. That is the right instinct and it is the single most useful thing already in place. It's not a mistake to tidy away — it's your future trunk extension, and it means the conversion is already underway.

What to do with it right now, in August

1. Don't cut it. Let it keep growing and go woody. Brown and stiff is what you want by autumn.

2. Tie it loosely to the high wire so it grows straight and doesn't whip or snap in wind. Soft tie, loose loop — it's going to thicken, so nothing tight.

3. Take any fruit off it. This shoot's job is to become wood, not to carry grapes.

4. If a vine has two or three going up, pick the best one — straightest, nearest the trunk, thickest. Keep one spare if you're unsure; remove the rest.

Because your vines are own-rooted, a shoot from low down is still the same grape — so it's perfectly good material for a new trunk. On a grafted vine it wouldn't be.

Then in late February — one decision, and don't get it wrong

If it reached the high wire AND is brown, woody and at least pencil-thick: cut it just above the wire. That's the cue for it to send out your two arms next season.

If it's thin, still greenish, or didn't reach: do not head it at the wire. Skinny wood makes weak arms and you'd be stuck with them for years. Instead either leave it tied full-length and head it the following winter, or cut it back to two or three buds and grow a stronger one next season.

The rule: only mature, pencil-thick-or-better brown wood becomes a trunk. Waiting a year costs you nothing. A weak trunk costs you a decade.

Meanwhile, leave the low arms alone and let them fruit. They keep working until the new high arms are actually carrying — that's the whole point of growing new arms instead of bending the old ones up.

01

Learn four things by sight

You cannot prune what you cannot name. Before cutting anything, find these four on a real vine. This is the slow part, and it is worth being slow at.

How to tell old wood from last year's cane

Last year's cane is smooth, tan, thin enough to bend in your hand, and has buds spaced along it. Older wood is darker, rougher, shaggy or peeling, and stiff.

Only last year's cane carries fruit. If you remove all of it, you get no grapes at all next year. That is the one mistake you cannot undo.

02

What a correct cut looks like

  • Sharp bypass secateurs. Blunt ones crush instead of cutting.
  • Late February into March — after the worst cold, as close to bud break as you can manage. Sap dripping from cuts is harmless.
  • Cut on a dry day if you can.
03

Where you are now, and where you're going

Where the fruit ends up — and what the lower wires are for

All the fruit sits in one band, just under the high wire — about 4–5 ft off the ground. Shoots grow off the arms and hang downward; clusters form near the top of that hanging curtain. Nothing fruits on the middle or lower wires.

The lower wires keep all their leaves. They stay green and clothing the fence — and they also comb the hanging shoots so the curtain stays tidy. They become leaf-and-shoot wires, never fruiting wires.

Why not fruit on both: two fruiting zones means double the crop, and the top canopy shades the bottom fruit. On a vine that already over-crops and already can't get past 6 Brix, that's the exact trap you're climbing out of.

During the changeover you'll still have low fruit — you don't strip the old low arms until the new high arms are carrying. Expect low clusters through 2027 and probably 2028. That's the plan working, not a mistake.

The thing that would break your vines

Do not try to bend the thick low arms up to the high wire. That wood will not bend — it cracks, and you lose the vine.

Instead: grow new arms up at the high wire, and retire the low ones gradually. Both researchers flagged this independently.

One reason this may matter more later: big trees keep growing. If the shade creeps earlier into the afternoon over the next few years, lifting the fruit higher and opening the canopy becomes more valuable, not less.

04

This winter, at each vine — four moves

  1. Pick one trunk. The straightest, healthiest stem, pencil-to-thumb thick, that already reaches the high wire or could. Cut the competing trunks off at the ground. If you're unsure about your pick, leave one thin backup and remove it next winter.
  2. Head it at the high wire. One cut, just above the wire. That tells the vine to send its two arms out along it.
  3. Rub off every sucker at the base and along the trunk. Do this with your thumb, not shears, and keep doing it all summer.
  4. Set the bud count using the table below — then walk away. Fewer buds than feels right is correct for this variety.

Next summer you'll pick two shoots along the high wire and tie them down as the permanent arms. The winter after that, you start cutting those arms back to short stubs — and from then on, pruning is the same job every year.

05

How many buds to leave

Seyval carries far more fruit than it can ripen, so it gets pruned much harder than a table grape. Don't do arithmetic in the cold — weigh, then read the row.

Rough-prune the vine first, leaving more than you need. Then weigh only the tan one-year-old canes you cut off — no trunk or arm wood. Kitchen or luggage scale. Find your weight below, leave that many buds, and stop.

Why this feels too severe

A Concord vine with 1.5 lb of prunings would keep 35 buds. Yours keeps 10. That gap is the single biggest reason generic grape advice will ruin a Seyval.

Expect to thin again in summer — this is normal

Seyval fruits from buds the formula doesn't count, so even a correct winter prune leaves too much crop. Removing more clusters in June and July is part of the job, not evidence you pruned wrong.

Write it down. Each vine: weight of prunings, buds left. That's the record that makes next winter easier.

06

The weak vines

Some vines will be noticeably weaker than their neighbours. Beginners get these wrong in both directions.

The rule

Lighter on fruit. Not lighter on buds.

Take every cluster off — a weak vine carries zero grapes this year. Tie a ribbon on it so you remember at bloom.

But leave 15–18 healthy buds if there's decent wood. The vine needs leaves to rebuild its roots.

If one is really feeble, treat it like a brand-new vine: cut back to a few strong buds and grow the trunk again.

Both mistakes are common: leaving fruit because the vine "needs something", and hard-pruning it because it looks small. Neither helps it.

If a whole side of the row is weak, look for a cause — not four unlucky vines

Two candidates, and both are worth checking before you blame the plants:

1. Water. Check the drip on the weak side — emitter output, blockages, how long that valve zone actually runs. Easiest thing to test, so test it first.

2. Tree roots. Big trees are the likely culprit if the weak vines are the ones nearest them. Tree roots run straight into watered, fertile ground and take water and nutrients before the vines get any. It is invisible from above and it is one of the most common reasons a garden vine underperforms next to mature trees.

How to check: dig a small hole 12–18″ down near a weak vine and look for fine tree roots threading through the soil. If they're there, the fixes are more water and feed on that side, or a root barrier — not harder pruning.

07

Never do these

  • Never cut the trunk you've chosen. You'd start over.
  • Never remove all the tan cane. That is next year's entire crop.
  • Never bend thick old arms upward. They crack.
  • Never leave lots of buds on a young vine because it looks bare. Bare is correct in March.
  • Never prune right before a hard freeze. Wait it out.

If you're unsure about a cut, don't make it. Take a photo, leave the cane, ask. An uncut cane can always be cut next week. A cut one can't be put back.
